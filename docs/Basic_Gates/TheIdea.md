Cutting the praises short, let's proceed with our computing journey. Last time we had seen how *Charles Babbage designed the idea for the steam engine but never materialized it*. However the revolution occurred almost half a century later with *Thomas Edison*.
As a matter of general knowledge we know that Thomas Edison had discovered the light bulb, but along with it, he had discovered another phenomenon which later was going to lay the foundation for possibly the most important discovery of all times(at least that's what I believe). Edison saw that one specific side of the glass of the bulb darkened after prolonged usage though the other side remained clear.This made him curious and led him towards the discovery of *thermionic effect and the directional flow of current*, in the sense that current could be directed in specific directions.
*This later led to the discovery of diodes and triodes which formed the basis of vacuum tubes*. Those who have taken a course in computers during their high school might get a bit nostalgic with this term. This was a term everyone heard but none understood. Yet, this formed the very first generation of digital computers, even the *ENIAC*.
But wait, we did actually overshoot a bit. We didn't derive the fundamentals. So let's go by the basics. Have you ever thought that if we have to represent everything we can think off, how many different symbols will we need? The thought experiment seems daunting and almost trivial at first sight, but this was the question *George Boole* answered in his work *The Laws of Thought* in the year *1854*. He stated that any human thought, no matter how complex, could be represented by a set of True and False statements connected by some logical statements (like and, or, not). For almost 100 years, this work remained just as abstract math which no one bothered about.
However in the year *1937*, *Claude Shanon*, found in it the solution that would bridge the gap between the digital and analogue, between the actual and virtual, between the human brain's intricacies and computer's massive executional power.
He saw that since any thought can be presented as a set of true and false statements, we would just need two physical states to represent any information. In electricity these two states can be illustrated by a switch(the ON state and the OFF state). This is when thoughts were mapped to circuits for the first time. 
This revelation started a spree of discoveries all across the globe and scientists started to contribute to this idea massively.However the switches though much more efficient than a bunch of gears, still didn't remove the mechanical aspect of computing. The physical switches still had to be toggled manually and worse, this toggling of switches couldn't be done by steam.
That's when the concept of diodes, triodes and vacuum tubes came to the rescue. Since they allowed flow of current only in single directions, they could be used as a switch which could effectively be toggled by electricity itself. However the downside of this was that vacuum tubes were power hungry and prone to failure. Their speed of toggling and massive sizes made computers a costly possession, something only the most prosperous governments could afford.
Then came *1947*, the year in which Bell Labs produced the first transistor. Transistors leveraged the natural property of semiconductors to counteract the demerits of vacuum tubes and this happened to be one of the most revolutionary discoveries of the *20th century*. Very soon after the Integrated circuits came into the scene, but that's a story we would deal with in the next chapter.
